The Sovereignty of Hope

record_voice_over Pastor David Thompson | schedule 45 mins
00:00

Let us turn our attention this morning to the concept of hope. Not the fragile, wishful thinking that the world offers, but a resilient, robust hope anchored in something unshakeable. When we speak of the sovereignty of God, we often think of His power, His majesty, His ultimate control over the tapestry of history. But how often do we connect that sovereignty directly to our daily, breathing hope?

If God is indeed sovereign—if not a single sparrow falls to the ground outside of His care—then our hope is not a gamble. It is an investment in a certainty. The scriptures tell us that hope deferred makes the heart sick, but a desire fulfilled is a tree of life. We live in an age of deferred hopes. We see it in the news, we feel it in our communities, we experience it in our own quiet moments of despair.

03:15

Consider the narrative of the exodus. The Israelites were given a promise. A land flowing with milk and honey. Yet, they found themselves wandering in a desolate wilderness for forty years. It is easy in the wilderness to believe that the promise has been forgotten. That the sovereign architect of the universe has simply moved on to other projects.

But the wilderness was not a detour; it was the path. It was the crucible in which a nation of slaves was forged into a people capable of receiving the promise. Their hope was tested, stripped of its earthly attachments, and forced to rely solely on the character of the One who promised.

This is where the sovereignty of hope becomes practical. It means that when you are in your wilderness—when the diagnosis comes, when the relationship fractures, when the silence of heaven seems deafening—your hope does not have to evaporate. Because your hope is not contingent on your circumstances; it is contingent on His character.

diamond
12:40

We often mistake hope for optimism. Optimism is the psychological disposition that things will get better. It is a useful trait, certainly, but it is deeply vulnerable to reality. When reality bites, optimism bleeds. Hope, however, is a theological virtue. It is the conviction that God is working all things together for good, even when the present moment is profoundly bad.

I am reminded of the words of the prophet Jeremiah. Writing from a place of deep national sorrow, witnessing the destruction of Jerusalem, he pens these words in Lamentations: "But this I call to mind, and therefore I have hope: The steadfast love of the Lord never ceases; his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning; great is your faithfulness."

"Hope, however, is a theological virtue. It is the conviction that God is working all things together for good, even when the present moment is profoundly bad."
21:05

Notice the pivot. He does not deny the ruin around him. He does not offer a platitude. He anchors himself in the unchanging nature of God. He calls to mind the character of the Sovereign. This is the discipline of hope. It is an active remembering.

So, how do we cultivate this? It begins with immersion in the narrative of scripture. We must read the old stories until they become our stories. We must see that the God who parted the sea, who shut the mouths of lions, who emptied the tomb, is the same God who walks with us in our Tuesday morning struggles.

Let us not be a people easily swayed by the wind. Let us be deeply rooted, holding fast to the confession of our hope without wavering, for He who promised is faithful. Amen.
